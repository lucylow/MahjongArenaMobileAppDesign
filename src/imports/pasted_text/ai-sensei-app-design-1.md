Design a mobile app called “AI Sensei,” an intelligent Mahjong game-review and coaching product for iPhone and Android. The app helps players import a completed four-player riichi Mahjong game, inspect critical decisions, understand shanten and scoring, and build a personalized training plan. Design it as a high-fidelity React Native + Expo mobile experience using a 390 x 844 frame, safe-area-aware layouts, and a compact bottom navigation labeled Dashboard, Review, Practice, Library, Profile.

The visual style should be calm, analytical, and trustworthy rather than flashy. Use a warm paper-white background, charcoal text, muted moss green, dark indigo, pale gold, and a precise coral-red accent for mistakes or danger. Combine editorial data visualization with tactile Mahjong details: miniature tile icons, clean timeline markers, thin dividers, soft cards, rounded corners, and restrained shadows. The app should feel like a personal coach and analysis notebook, not a gambling app.

Create these connected screens:

1. Coaching dashboard. Show a greeting such as “Good evening, Alex,” a weekly improvement score, review streak, current focus area, and a primary button labeled “Review a Game.” Add three progress cards: “Tile Efficiency,” “Defensive Discards,” and “Scoring Decisions.” Include a recommendation such as “You are improving at early-hand efficiency; this week, practice reading dangerous discards.”

2. Import game screen. Provide large input cards for Tenhou URL, Mahjong Soul URL, Riichi City log ID, and Custom Game Log. Include a target-player selector with East, South, West, and North. Add an “Import and Analyze” button, an explanatory privacy note, and an empty state showing a sample game-log preview. Make the form feel simple and approachable. Include a small “Supported formats” disclosure instead of overwhelming technical details.

3. Review overview. After analysis, show a game summary with final placement, score change, total hands, and a visual timeline of each kyoku. Each timeline item should show the round, result, score delta, and a color-coded decision-quality indicator. Add filters for “All,” “High Impact,” “Defensive,” “Scoring,” and “Learning Moments.” Include a clear notice that an AI rating is model-dependent and should be treated as a coaching signal rather than an absolute truth.

4. What-would-you-do moment. Create the core review screen. Show the Mahjong table state at a selected decision point, including the player’s hand, discards, open melds, dora indicator, round wind, scores, and turn number. Below the table, display a prompt: “What would you discard?” with selectable tile choices. After selection, reveal the AI comparison. Show the player’s choice, the recommended choice, estimated EV or expected outcome, shanten change, ukeire change, deal-in risk, and a concise explanation. Use progressive disclosure so advanced statistics are hidden under “Show deeper analysis.”

5. Decision detail sheet. Create a bottom sheet titled “Why this recommendation?” with a simple three-layer explanation: “Hand shape,” “Opponent danger,” and “Future value.” Use a horizontal probability visualization rather than a dense table. Include example text such as: “Discarding 7-Man keeps two useful acceptance tiles and preserves a faster route to tenpai. The AI also estimates that 9-Sou is more dangerous because of the visible tile pattern.” Clearly label all values as estimates.

6. Personal training plan. Show a weekly plan generated from review history. Include practice cards such as “Choose safe folds,” “Compare 1-shanten shapes,” “Recognize tanyao opportunities,” and “Calculate han and fu.” Each card should have a difficulty label, estimated duration, progress state, and “Start Drill” button. Add a small conversational coach card from Sensei: “You often choose high-value lines late in the hand. Let’s practice when speed is more important than maximum value.”

7. Practice drill screen. Show a single Mahjong decision with four answer choices, instant feedback, a confidence slider, and a “See expert reasoning” button. Include progress at the top, a calm success state, and a non-punitive explanation for incorrect answers. End with “Add to Review Queue” and “Next Drill.”

8. Settings and model transparency. Show AI engine selection, analysis depth, language, tile notation preferences, data retention, delete imported logs, and a “How the AI works” page. Explain in plain language that the system evaluates visible game state, inferred opponent possibilities, hand efficiency, scoring potential, and risk. Do not expose fake precision or claim perfect play.

Use realistic Mahjong vocabulary such as shanten, ukeire, yaku, fu, han, dora, honba, riichi, and deal-in, but pair each technical term with a short explanation or tooltip. Use charts sparingly and make them readable on a small screen. Prioritize one important decision per screen. Avoid overly gamified badges, cluttered spreadsheets, inaccessible red/green-only feedback, and unexplained numerical ratings.
